# Social Studies Units 1–3: source and authoring record

Checked September 7, 2026. Scope: `social-studies-u01-l01` through `social-studies-u03-l06` (18 lessons, 54 cards, 234 explicitly authored questions).

All teaching paragraphs, examples, tips, questions, explanations, worked examples, and activity summaries are newly authored for Cram All. Activity source texts visibly say “Newly authored summary.” No passage presented as a summary is a quotation. Pip and kid lines are fictional coaching, not historical testimony. Primary documents below are used through labeled summaries; their original discriminatory terminology is not reproduced. Later institutional accounts are identified as historical interpretations or context, not as witnesses.

The normative allocation uses the [2019 South Carolina Social Studies standards](https://www.ed.sc.gov/instruction/standards/social-studies/standards/2019-south-carolina-social-studies-college-and-career-ready-standards/), Grade 4 printed pages 28–37. Content and skill scope were checked against the [May 2024 Grade 4 alignment guide](https://www.ed.sc.gov/instruction/standards/social-studies/instructional-resources/grade-4-us-sc-studies-part-i-alignment-guide-2024-may-2024/), printed pages 8–21, 23–35, and 37–49. The guide is context for the 2019 standards, not a new standards edition. Its lists include possible examples; lessons teach the complete indicator skill and substantive topics without pretending three short cards contain every possible historical example.

## Unit 1: People and Colonies, 1600–1730

### social-studies-u01-l01 — When Cultures Met — 4.1.CO

Coverage: compare Native/European encounters in Plymouth and Carolina; connect Native knowledge and agency with English, French, and Spanish colonial goals; distinguish indentured and enslaved labor; African cultural contributions and northern/southern slavery.

- [NPS, Powhatan](https://www.nps.gov/people/powhatan.htm) and [Colonial National Historical Park](https://www.nps.gov/articles/colonial.htm): authoritative accounts of trade, food, tools, authority, and deteriorating relations at Jamestown.
- [Plimoth Patuxet Museums, Unit 2](https://plimoth.org/yath/unit-2): the 1621 Ousamequin–Plymouth protection agreement and later land pressures leading to the 1675 war. The activity compares the agreement with Carolina trade rather than inventing a treaty shared by both.
- [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Yamassee War](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/yamassee-war/): trade debt, settler encroachment, and the 1715 conflict. Non-graphic summary; no suggestion that trade alone caused the war.
- [NPS, Life in the Missions](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/travelspanishmissions/life-in-the-missions-between-reality-romance-and-revolt.htm): religious conversion, controlled work, and Native resistance in New Spain. French fur trade and the labor-system comparison are also covered by the SCDE guide, 4.1.CO.
- [NPS, Gullah Geechee Communities](https://www.nps.gov/timu/learn/historyculture/gullah-geechee-communities.htm): African roots, cultural continuity, coastal context.

Simplifications: “Powhatan communities” distinguishes people from the paramount chief. “Native nations” does not imply one culture or a uniform response. Cooperation is not described as consent to colonial occupation. Indentures are described as normally having an expected term, not a guarantee of humane treatment.

### social-studies-u01-l02 — Trade and Forced Labor — 4.1.CE

Coverage: mercantilist trade restrictions and raw/finished goods; plantation and headright incentives; Barbados–Carolina trade; Atlantic routes/Middle Passage; unequal effects on Europeans, Africans, and Native communities.

- [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Land granting](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/land-granting/): headrights rewarded enslavers, not enslaved workers, and encouraged large holdings.
- [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Slavery](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/slavery/): early Carolina supplies to Barbados, rice skills, forced labor.
- [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Slave trade](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/slave-trade/): direct African trade, Caribbean routes, and Charles Town’s role.
- [Library of Congress, Colonies in America: Commerce, Business, and the Economy](https://guides.loc.gov/colonial-america-business-research): broader colonial commercial context; SCDE guide 4.1.CE supplies the explicit mercantilism/triangular-trade scope.

Simplifications: triangular trade is expressly a schematic label, not one route followed by every ship. People are not called goods even when explaining records that treated them as property. The cotton-to-cloth example explicitly teaches the raw-material/finished-product relationship assessed by q02.

### social-studies-u01-l03 — Life in Three Colonial Regions — 4.1.P

Coverage: development of regional political institutions and social/religious characteristics, including town meetings, Mayflower Compact, assemblies, House of Burgesses, proprietors, limited voting, Quaker/Puritan differences, geography and slavery.

- [Library of Congress, Geography and Its Impact on Colonial Life](https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/geography-and-its-impact-on-colonial-life/): regional resources and work patterns.
- [Library of Congress, America as a Religious Refuge](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel01.html): migration for religion and regional religious communities.
- [Plimoth Patuxet, Who Were the Pilgrims?](https://plimoth.org/for-students/homework-help/who-were-the-pilgrims): Plymouth’s community and Wampanoag context.
- SCDE guide 4.1.P, pages 12–13: colonial institutions, religions, regional identities, and limits on self-government.

Simplifications: the three-region map is explicitly schematic and about 1700. Regional patterns are not universal descriptions; the South also had small farms and the North also had slavery. The House of Burgesses is accurately located in Virginia, not South Carolina.

### social-studies-u01-l04 — People of Early South Carolina — 4.1.CX

Coverage: Catawba/Piedmont, Cherokee/mountain, and Yamasee/coastal contexts; port connections; African skill and forced labor; indentures and Barbados; proprietors, constitution proposal, religious communities, and living Gullah Geechee culture.

- [Catawba Nation, About the Nation](https://catawba.com/about-the-nation): the nation’s own account of river farming, homelands, and colonial trade routes.
- [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Charleston](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/charleston/): 1670 founding, 1680 move, mixed communities and Atlantic port.
- [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Immigration](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/immigration/): Barbados connections and religious tolerance as a recruitment policy.
- [Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina, 1669, Yale Avalon transcription](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/17th_century/nc05.asp): primary-source proposal, especially religious provisions and enslavement. Editorial note confirms only partial implementation.
- [NPS, Gullah Geechee Culture](https://www.nps.gov/fofr/learn/historyculture/gullah-geechee-culture.htm): rice expertise and continuing language, family, food, and cultural traditions.

Simplifications: map locations indicate broad historical contexts, not exact settlement footprints or current tribal boundaries. The complete Fundamental Constitutions plan is not presented as enacted law. The same harbor can involve voluntary opportunity and forced arrival.

### social-studies-u01-l05 — How Colonial Work Changed — 4.1.CC

Coverage: continuity in farming and trade alongside growing export agriculture, harbor development, rice knowledge, northern/southern work, continued slavery and resistance.

- [NPS, John Rolfe](https://home.nps.gov/jame/learn/historyculture/john-rolfe.htm) and [Tobacco: The Early History of a New World Crop](https://www.nps.gov/jame/learn/historyculture/tobacco-the-early-history-of-a-new-world-crop.htm): 1612 experiments and later export growth; Native tobacco growing predates Rolfe.
- [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Charleston](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/charleston/): 1670 and 1680 dated port evidence.
- [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Agriculture](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/agriculture/) and [Slavery](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/slavery/): early supplies, cash-crop development, and labor systems.
- SCDE guide 4.1.CC: economic patterns, resistance, and northern/southern slavery.

Simplifications: indigo’s importance in the 1740s is labeled later than the unit’s main period. A timeline orders actual dated events; explanation connects those events with the continuing export economy. Economic growth is not equated with workers’ freedom.

### social-studies-u01-l06 — Clues to Colonial Life — 4.1.E

Coverage: primary/secondary sources, author/purpose, opposing perspectives on colonial politics, labor and society; substantiated claims and missing voices.

- [Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina, 1669](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/17th_century/nc05.asp), especially sections 107 and 110: primary proposal defending enslavers’ power.
- [Samuel Sewall, The Selling of Joseph, 1700](https://slaverylawpower.org/chapters/whig-moments/samuel-sewall-selling-joseph-1700/): primary pamphlet transcription, credited by its edition to the Massachusetts Historical Society copy. It argues against buying and selling people and invokes shared liberty.
- [MHS collections guide](https://www.masshist.org/collection-guides/view/fas0265?terms=civil+war): confirms the pamphlet, its 1700 date and provenance. Direct MHS object endpoint `/database/53` failed to load during checking; the institutional collections description and scholarly primary-text edition were inspected instead.

Simplifications: a brief summary of Sewall’s antislavery argument is not a claim that he held modern equality views in every respect. Opposing documents do not stand for all colonists. Neither source is mislabeled as an enslaved person’s account, and a pamphlet is not treated as a law that ended slavery.

## Unit 2: Building a New Nation, 1730–1800

### social-studies-u02-l01 — Many People in the Revolution — 4.2.CO

Coverage: compare women’s, free/enslaved African Americans’, and Native peoples’ economic, military, support and political roles; differing goals of security, independence and personal freedom.

- [National Women’s History Museum, Deborah Sampson](https://www.womenshistory.org/education-resources/biographies/deborah-sampson): enlistment under a man’s name and service. The lesson avoids the disputed Yorktown-service story.
- [NPS, African Americans in the American Revolution](https://www.nps.gov/teachers/classrooms/african-americans-in-the-american-revolution.htm) and [Patriots of Color at Valley Forge](https://www.nps.gov/vafo/learn/historyculture/patriotsofcoloratvalleyforge.htm): both sides, varied work, freedom-seeking and conditional promises.
- [NPS, Crispus Attucks](https://home.nps.gov/people/crispus-attucks.htm): sailor of African and Native ancestry and Boston Massacre victim. The lesson does not assert an uncertain legal status at his death.
- [NPS, The Oneida in the American Revolution](https://www.nps.gov/articles/the-oneida-nation-in-the-american-revolution.htm): guides, scouts, information, soldiers, alliance choices and damage to communities.

Simplifications: membership in a group does not assign a single loyalty. “Many Oneida” avoids universalizing. Military contributions do not imply equal voting rights, protected homelands, or universal emancipation.

### social-studies-u02-l02 — Why Colonists Sought Independence — 4.2.CE

Coverage: war debt, taxes and representation; Tea Act/company privilege, protests and Coercive Acts; Patriots/Loyalists; Continental Army, attempted peace, Declaration; interacting economic/political motivations.

- SCDE guide 4.2.CE, pages 25–26, for the full linked content and causal inquiry.
- [NPS, The Olive Branch Petition](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/the-olive-branch-petition.htm): 1775 reconciliation attempt, rejection, and independence context.
- [NPS, Boston Massacre Trial](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/boston-massacre-trial.htm): five people killed and subsequent legal proceedings.
- [National Archives, Declaration of Independence transcription](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/declaration-transcript): primary complaints and declaration of independence.

Simplifications: the Tea Act did not create a new tea tax; the lesson correctly says it kept a disputed tax. Independence is the result of multiple events, debates and decisions, not the inevitable or unanimous response to one event.

### social-studies-u02-l03 — Building Our Government — 4.2.P

Coverage: Continental Congresses, independence and treaty, Articles limitations, proposal/ratification/operation, democratic republic, federalism, branches/checks, preamble goals and unequal political rights.

- [National Archives, Articles of Confederation](https://www.archives.gov/historical-docs/articles-of-confederation): 1781 operation and first constitution.
- [National Archives, Constitution history](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/more-perfect-union): 1787 proposal, 1788 ninth-state ratification, 1789 government, debates and power structures.
- [National Archives, Bill of Rights transcription](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights-transcript): 1791 amendments.
- [NPS, The Constitution and the Underground Railroad](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/the-constitution-and-the-underground-railroad.htm): political benefits to slave states from constitutional protection of slavery.
- SCDE guide 4.2.P, pages 27–28, supplies Continental Congress, Paine, Treaty of Paris, Washington, and constitutional scope.

Simplifications: dates support sequence rather than isolated recall. Ratification and signing are distinct. Three-fifths is explained as population counting for representation/direct taxation, not a measure of human worth and not a grant of votes to enslaved people.

### social-studies-u02-l04 — South Carolina and Independence — 4.2.CX

Coverage: South Carolina’s divided loyalties, coastal and inland campaign context, Marion/Sumter/Pickens, Cowpens, state constitution/assembly and national political influence.

- [NPS, The Southern Campaign](https://www.nps.gov/cowp/learn/historyculture/southerncampaign.htm) and [The Battle of Cowpens](https://www.nps.gov/cowp/learn/historyculture/the-battle-of-cowpens.htm): 1776 defense, 1780 Charleston defeat, 1781 inland victory and wider effects.
- NPS biographies: [Francis Marion](https://www.nps.gov/people/francis-marion.htm), [Thomas Sumter](https://www.nps.gov/people/thomas-sumter.htm), [Andrew Pickens](https://www.nps.gov/people/andrew-pickens.htm).
- [U.S. House, Presidents of the Continental and Confederation Congresses](https://history.house.gov/People/Continental-Congress/Presidents/): Henry Middleton briefly presided in October 1774.
- [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Edward Rutledge](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/rutledge-edward/) and [Constitutions](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/constitutions/): Declaration participation and state government.
- [NPS, The Constitution and the Underground Railroad](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/the-constitution-and-the-underground-railroad.htm): Charles Pinckney and slavery protections.

Simplifications: the map uses broad harbor/battlefield markers, not a tactical simulation. Cowpens is a major contribution within a broader campaign, not a single event that won the whole war. Leaders’ political contributions do not hide their defense of slavery.

### social-studies-u02-l05 — Rights and Promises — 4.2.CC

Coverage: natural-rights claims before and after the French and Indian War, tighter imperial control, searches/quartering/taxation/trials, Declaration, amendments, ongoing exclusions.

- [Declaration of Independence](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/declaration-transcript): primary rights claims and complaints about consent, trials, and quartering.
- [Bill of Rights](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights-transcript): primary First, Second, Third, Fourth and trial amendments; original federal scope is explained in the lesson.
- [NPS, Valley Forge guide transcript](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/vafo-cell-phone-guide-transcript.htm): civilian experiences of troop quartering and the later Third Amendment connection.
- SCDE guide 4.2.CC, pages 31–32, for salutary neglect and the full continuity/change frame.

Simplifications: the 1765 Quartering Act is described as requiring housing/support, not a blanket order to occupy every private home. The First Amendment is explicitly named before the question asking about it. Written protections are distinguished from actual equal rights.

### social-studies-u02-l06 — Different Views of a New Nation — 4.2.E

Coverage: compare competing views of representation and national power; economic/social diversity, source purpose, ratification, missing voices and limited conclusions.

- [James Madison, Federalist No. 10, 1787](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/federalist-10/): primary argument that a larger republic and varied interests can resist domination by factions.
- [Brutus I, 1787](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/brutus-i/): primary argument that a large territory and differing needs threaten adequate representation and state control.
- [Teaching American History, The Debate over Ratification](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/collections/the-debate-over-ratification/): editorial context for the paired texts.
- [National Archives, Constitution history](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/more-perfect-union): later approval/rights context.

Simplifications: Brutus is correctly a pen name; no contested author identity is required. Madison’s claim is not treated as proof of all later outcomes. Two essays cannot establish everyone’s beliefs, especially those excluded from equal political influence.

## Unit 3: A Growing and Divided Country, 1800–1850

### social-studies-u03-l01 — Moving West, Different Experiences — 4.3.CO

Coverage: expedition/migration goals, Native responses and assistance, voluntary/forced travel, cotton/mining/farming/trade motives, expansionist claims and unequal consequences.

- [NPS, The Lewis and Clark Expedition](https://www.nps.gov/mnrr/learn/historyculture/the-lewis-and-clark-expedition.htm), [Lewis and Clark at Knife River](https://home.nps.gov/knri/learn/historyculture/lewis-and-clark.htm), and [Sacagawea](https://www.nps.gov/lecl/learn/historyculture/sacagawea.htm): route, Mandan/Hidatsa villages, interpretation, Shoshone assistance and horses.
- [NPS, The Expedition’s Impact](https://home.nps.gov/articles/the-expeditions-impact.htm): expansion and later costs to Native communities.
- [California State Parks, Gold Rush overview](https://demo2.parks.ca.gov/?page_id=1081) and [travelers on the southern route](https://www.parks.ca.gov/?page_id=24680): mining and later farming/business migration motives.
- [National Archives, Cotton Gin patent](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/patent-for-cotton-gin): cotton and forced labor expansion; SCDE 4.3.CO for Manifest Destiny and Monroe Doctrine context.

Simplifications: Sacagawea is not described as single-handedly guiding the entire route; her supported interpretation and regional knowledge are named. Native help did not cede sovereignty. Map markers follow the east-to-upper-Missouri-to-Pacific journey on a present-day reference outline, not historical borders.

### social-studies-u03-l02 — Land, Laws, and Forced Removal — 4.3.CE

Coverage: purchase, transport support, removal policy and resistance, Mexican cession, slave/free-state compromises and capture laws.

- [National Archives, Louisiana Purchase Treaty](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/louisiana-purchase-treaty): 1803 transfer of France’s territorial claim.
- [Library of Congress, Railroad Land Grants](https://www.loc.gov/collections/railroad-maps-1828-to-1900/articles-and-essays/history-of-railroads-and-maps/land-grants/): government grants supporting railroad construction beginning in 1850. This is not misdated as the later 1862 transcontinental act.
- [National Archives, Jackson’s removal message](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/jacksons-message-to-congress-on-indian-removal): 1830 policy, contested treaty, and 1838–1839 forced Cherokee removal.
- [NMAI, Cherokee removal exhibition](https://americanindian.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/item?id=967) and [1836 petition transcription](https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360/removal-cherokee/transcripts/pdf/petition-original.pdf): Cherokee perspective and resistance.
- National Archives: [Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/treaty-of-guadalupe-hidalgo), [Missouri Compromise](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/missouri-compromise), [Compromise of 1850](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/compromise-of-1850).

Simplifications: the treaty was signed by a minority whose authority most Cherokee people rejected; it is not presented as consent by the nation. The main forced Cherokee removal is dated 1838–1839 (some educational source captions use inconsistent earlier dates). Missouri’s actual state entry was 1821; the lesson names the 1820 compromise’s arrangement, not an incorrect exact entry date.

### social-studies-u03-l03 — New Tools Changed the Land — 4.3.P

Coverage: technology as a turning point in work, migration/communication and environmental scale, including cotton/slavery, prairie farms, steam transport, telegraph and mining.

- [National Archives, Patent for Cotton Gin](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/patent-for-cotton-gin): 1794 patent and later expansion of cotton land and enslaved labor.
- [Smithsonian, John Deere Plow](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_857013) and [museum tactile description](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/visual-descriptions/visual-description-in-pursuit/john-deere-plow-tactile): the specific surviving plow dates to 1838; steel improved work in sticky prairie soils and reshaped land.
- [Library of Congress, First telegraph message, May 24, 1844](https://www.loc.gov/item/mcc00054/): primary message record and Washington–Baltimore transmission.
- [California State Parks, Gold Rush chronology](https://www.parks.ca.gov/?page_id=1099), [Conifer Connection: Human History](https://www.parks.ca.gov/pages/735/files/Human%20History%20ADA.pdf), and [California Geological Survey/State Parks, Geological Gems](https://www.parks.ca.gov/pages/734/files/GeoGems_All.pdf): 1848 discovery/1849 rush, resource pressure, later hydraulic mining damage.

Simplifications: 1794 is explicitly prior context for later effects. The timeline dates an actual 1838 artifact, not the first invention of a steel plow. Later hydraulic mining is explicitly after the main 1800–1850 period, and late Klondike mining is not used as evidence of 1849 California methods.

### social-studies-u03-l04 — Why Regions Disagreed — 4.3.CX

Coverage: antebellum sectionalism, connected regional economies, tariffs and nullification, South Carolina leaders’ defense of slavery and Southern-born abolitionists, national enforcement and resistance.

- [U.S. Senate, John C. Calhoun](https://www.senate.gov/senators/FeaturedBios/Featured_Bio_Calhoun.htm) and [South Carolina Nullification Ordinance, DocsTeach](https://docsteach.org/document/south-carolina-nullification-ordinance/): political doctrine and the 1832 tariff dispute.
- [NPS, Grimke Sisters](https://www.nps.gov/wori/learn/historyculture/grimke-sisters.htm) and [Angelina Grimké](https://home.nps.gov/people/angelina-emily-grimk%C3%A9-weld.htm): South Carolina childhood, rejection of slavery, speaking/writing and women’s rights.
- [National Archives, Compromise of 1850](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/compromise-of-1850): free-state admission and stronger fugitive-slave enforcement.
- SCDE guide 4.3.CX, pages 43–44: Jackson, tariff/farming context, Tubman, Grimké and abolition.

Simplifications: tariff economics and slavery are connected but not called identical causes. Northern paid labor does not erase Northern profits from slavery. Tubman’s 1849 escape is distinguished from her later assistance to other freedom seekers.

### social-studies-u03-l05 — When Borders Moved — 4.3.CC

Coverage: Spanish-speaking and Native communities’ changed government/land conditions alongside enduring culture, distinct Native resistance/removal cases, promises versus treatment.

- [NPS, Californio to American](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/upload/TWHP-Lessons_8californio.pdf): 1821 Mexican setting, 1848 transfer and continuity/change in established communities.
- [National Archives, Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/treaty-of-guadalupe-hidalgo): 1848 cession, property and citizenship provisions; the present southern border also includes a later 1853 acquisition and is not labeled the exact 1848 line.
- [NPS, American Latino Theme Study: Law](https://www.nps.gov/articles/latinothemestudylaw.htm) and [Afro-Latinidad on the Anza Trail](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/afro-latinidad-on-the-anza-trail.htm): later legal costs, land loss and discrimination.
- [Catawba Nation, About the Nation](https://catawba.com/about-the-nation): 1840 Nations Ford land loss and continuing community; National Archives removal sources above provide Cherokee comparison. SCDE 4.3.CC supplies Seminole/Osceola and later assimilation context.

Representation decision accepted by the parent: the activity compares dated 1821/1848 territorial and community **text evidence** at geographic markers. It does not render two historical boundary maps. Every relevant visible label says the outline is present-day geographic reference, not historical borders. California and the historical New Mexico region are distinguished from exact modern state boundaries. Later Carlisle (1879) is expressly outside the main period. Cultural continuity is never equated with absence of coercion or loss.

### social-studies-u03-l06 — Looking West Through Different Eyes — 4.3.E

Coverage: multiple primary-source perspectives on removal and expansion; policy argument versus consent/outcome; slave/free-state additions, political bargaining and missing voices.

- [Andrew Jackson’s 1830 message, National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/jacksons-message-to-congress-on-indian-removal): primary argument praising removal and white settlement.
- [Cherokee Nation 1836 protest petition, NMAI transcription](https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360/removal-cherokee/transcripts/pdf/petition-original.pdf): primary opposition to unauthorized treaty signers and defense of rights. The one-page transcript was inspected.
- [National Archives, Missouri Compromise](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/missouri-compromise) and [Compromise of 1850](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/compromise-of-1850): primary legislative evidence plus authoritative context on political balance, California and freedom-seeker capture.

Simplifications: Jackson’s assertions are explicitly attributed claims; demeaning terminology is omitted rather than normalized. Cherokee petitioners’ opposition is central. A law about slavery does not prove enslaved people agreed to it. “Free state” describes legal prohibition of slavery, not equal rights or guaranteed safety.

## Content verification

Each lesson has three core paragraphs of 76–90 words in this wave, with concrete examples, short tips, a worked example, 13 distinct authored questions, multiple-choice and true/false types, and a two-turn guide/kid activity introduction with retry, milestone, and completion reactions. Activity references resolve under the shared strict schemas. All 234 questions map to the card whose visible teaching, example, or tip supports their answer; the helper retains those complete notes in Quick Check references. Dates principally support sequence and comparison rather than isolated date memorization.

Unit tests were written before the corresponding lesson modules and observed failing on missing imports. After authoring, all three focused unit suites pass. Parent owns shared integration, broader regression/build gates, browser verification, and final independent acceptance; this record does not claim those gates complete.
