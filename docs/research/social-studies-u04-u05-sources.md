# Social Studies Units 4–5: lesson provenance

Prepared September 7, 2026 for the approved [Social Studies design](../superpowers/specs/2026-09-07-social-studies-design.md) and [implementation plan, Task 4](../superpowers/plans/2026-09-07-social-studies.md).

Scope: `social-studies-u04-l01` through `social-studies-u05-l06`, authored in `src/content/social-studies/u04.ts` and `u05.ts`. All learner prose and activity sources are newly written summaries. No historical speech, diary, letter, or witness account has been invented. Pip's dialogue is present-day instructional coaching. No direct historical quotations are used, apart from historical document titles, established historical labels, and the brief phrase “new birth of freedom.”

## Normative and instructional sources

- [SCDE, 2019 South Carolina Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards](https://www.ed.sc.gov/instruction/standards/social-studies/standards/2019-south-carolina-social-studies-college-and-career-ready-standards/), printed pages 35–37: exact indicators 4.4.CO/CE/P/CX/CC/E and 4.5.CO/CE/P/CX/CC/E. These govern the lesson allocations.
- [SCDE, Grade 4 United States and South Carolina Studies Part I Alignment Guide, May 2024](https://www.ed.sc.gov/instruction/standards/social-studies/instructional-resources/grade-4-us-sc-studies-part-i-alignment-guide-2024-may-2024/), printed pages 50–79: reviewed the complete Standard 4 and 5 context, content lists, inquiry questions, and evidence tasks. Examples are instructional possibilities, not a requirement to assess every listed name or date.

The alignment guide contains some apparent errors in examples: an “Andrew Jackson” Reconstruction plan, the First Reconstruction Act dated 1866, and the Fifteenth Amendment dated 1868. The lessons use **Andrew Johnson**, **1867 Reconstruction Acts**, and **1870 ratification of the Fifteenth Amendment**, verified against the authoritative sources below. The document proposed by Congress and its eventual ratification are distinct events. None of these corrections changes an official indicator's wording or allocation.

| SC-Women | [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Women](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/women/) — scholarly state reference | Different legal, racial, and economic positions of women; wartime responsibilities. Broad examples are marked as comparisons, not fictional diary accounts. |

No graphic medical detail is reproduced. |

The lesson makes no unsupported universal claim that all northern businesses prospered. |
| Reconstruction | [NPS, Reconstruction, by Eric Foner](https://www.nps.gov/articles/reconstruction.htm) — authoritative historical essay | Lincoln and Johnson plans, congressional Reconstruction, Black civic action and white allies, business interests, schools, Black Codes, opposition, enforcement, and 1877. |
| Bureau | [U.S. Senate, Freedmen's Bureau Acts of 1865 and 1866](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/generic/FreedmensBureau.htm) — official legislative history | Federal aid, labor agreements, education, Johnson's veto, congressional action, and limits on the agency. |

| SC-Constitution | [South Carolina Encyclopedia, Constitutions](https://www.scencyclopedia.org/sce/entries/constitutions/) — scholarly state reference | Charleston's 1868 convention; Black and white delegates, male voting, public schools and implementation limits; Columbia's role as state capital. |

The lesson uses labeled paraphrase, not invented quotations. |

## Per-lesson provenance and instructional decisions

| Lesson | Indicator and period | Source keys | Paraphrase and simplification decisions |
|---|---|---|---|
| `social-studies-u04-l01` — Slavery and the Road to War | 4.4.CO; principally 1850–1861 | CW-Causes, SC-Declaration | Compares regional economic patterns with laws and power. Slavery is explicitly central, northern economic connections are included, and neither region is treated as one unanimous population. Brief summaries cover the Compromise of 1850, Kansas–Nebraska, Dred Scott, Harpers Ferry, election, secession, and Fort Sumter. Separates an underlying cause from the opening attack. |
| `social-studies-u04-l02` — How War Plans Affected People | 4.4.CE; 1861–1865 | CW-Strategy, CW-Conscription, Hunley | Explains both Union and Confederate strategy, named geographic routes, technology, conscription, and Sherman through effects on supplies and people. The cause/effect activity builds four relationships from visible geographic source notes. A single encounter is not presented as determining the whole war. Civilian costs are direct and non-graphic. |
| `social-studies-u04-l03` — People Worked for Freedom | 4.4.P; 1850–1865 | Tubman, Scotts, Douglass-War, Emancipation, Amendment-13, CW-Causes | Civic action includes petitions, speeches, organizing, escape, court action, and service. Sequence includes a setback, not inevitable progress. Explains emancipation as a turning point and preserves the proclamation's geographic/enforcement limits and the amendment's punishment exception. The Underground Railroad is explicitly a network, not a train. |
| `social-studies-u04-l04` — South Carolinians During the War | 4.4.CX; 1861–1865 | Planter, Port-Royal, SC-Women, CW-Conscription, CW-Strategy, Tubman | Compares African American agency, women's differing work and circumstances, and planter power in South Carolina. The Planter account includes the crew and families. Forten and Tubman have distinct roles. Large-plantation exemptions and poorer families' unequal burdens contextualize the planter class. Composite comparisons are phrased as possibilities, not attributed witness reports. |
| `social-studies-u04-l05` — How the War Changed Life | 4.4.CC; 1860–1865 with immediate aftermath | CW-Strategy, Cotton-Mills, Emancipation, Amendment-13, Barton, Truth | Covers economic, political, and social change and continuity. Avoids “all northern industry prospered,” recognizes new and continuing responsibilities, and evaluates abolition alongside persisting inequality. Soldiers' lasting needs are included without graphic detail. The timeline compares legal status and policy rather than rating slavery or war as a game. |
| `social-studies-u04-l06` — Evidence from a Divided Nation | 4.4.E; sources concerning 1860 and 1863 | SC-Declaration, Douglass-War | Compares a primary declaration in summary with a clearly labeled later historical account of Douglass's wartime perspective. Explains opposite goals about slavery, labor, government power, and status. Questions require purpose and comparison, not just assigning isolated facts. Limits claims to the represented perspectives rather than every resident of each region. |
| `social-studies-u05-l01` — People Rebuilding Communities | 4.5.CO; 1863–1870 | Reconstruction, Bureau, Edisto-Howard | Compares freedpeople, northern and southern allies, federal workers, business leaders, former landowners, and political leaders. Includes Lincoln, Johnson, and congressional approaches. Hostile terms “carpetbagger” and “scalawag” are identified as opponents' labels rather than neutral judgments. Emphasizes community agency and competing interests over work and land. |
| `social-studies-u05-l02` — Three Changes to the Constitution | 4.5.CE; 1865–1870 with enforcement context | Amendment-13, Amendment-14, Amendment-15, Civil-Rights-1866, Reconstruction | Gives each amendment its own effects and limits. Preserves the Thirteenth's exception, Fourteenth's jurisdiction condition and historical Native citizenship limits, and Fifteenth's failure to address sex-based exclusion. Explains ratification separately from proposal and distinguishes written rights from access to rights in practice. |
| `social-studies-u05-l03` — A Turning Point After War | 4.5.P; 1865–1877 | Amendment-13, Reconstruction, Rights-Barriers, Port-Royal | Connects changes in labor and government: wage work, sharecropping, debt, federal supervision, Black male participation, coalitions, and schools. Sharecropping is distinguished from slavery while its unequal bargaining power is explained. Describes 1877 as ending support for the remaining Reconstruction governments, not all federal troops leaving the entire South or all Black political participation ending instantly. |
| `social-studies-u05-l04` — Rebuilding South Carolina | 4.5.CX; 1861–1877 | Port-Royal, SC-Constitution, Chamberlain, Rights-Barriers | Explicitly covers the Port Royal Experiment and state Constitution of 1868, with economic, labor, political, and social conditions. Separates Charleston's convention from inland Columbia's lawmaking and the Beaufort/Sea Islands setting. School promises are distinguished from adequate facilities and access. The map is north-up and labeled a schematic, not to scale. |
| `social-studies-u05-l05` — New Rights, Unfair Barriers | 4.5.CC; 1865–1877, explicitly labeled later Jim Crow context | Amendment-13, Amendment-14, Amendment-15, Civil-Rights-1866, Reconstruction, Rights-Barriers, Port-Royal | Compares gains, Black Codes, intimidation, federal enforcement, the 1877 reversal, and continued resistance. Names white supremacy, Klan and Red Shirt suppression without graphic detail. “Redeemers” is treated as a self-description. States that Jim Crow developed over later decades, rather than appearing fully formed in 1877. Links institutions and amendments to later civil-rights efforts. |
| `social-studies-u05-l06` — What Reconstruction Changed | 4.5.E; primary accounts from 1865–1866 | Edisto-Howard, Douglass-Reconstruction | Three genuinely different arguments: freedpeople's land/home security, Howard's agreements within property-restoration policy, and Douglass's national argument for voting power. Preserves agreement and disagreement between the committee and Howard and adds a northern advocate's perspective. Identifies the earlier committee appeal preceding Howard's reply and the later presidential petition. Distinguishes demands, advice, and verified outcomes. |

## Map and activity checks

The Unit 5 Lesson 4 map uses the shared widget's 0–100 coordinate convention, north up. Beaufort/Sea Islands `(52,86)` is southwest of Charleston `(70,72)`; Columbia `(50,48)` is inland and northwest of Charleston. These relative positions were checked against the geographic descriptions in the NPS Port Royal material and the state-constitution history. The Beaufort marker labels a regional cluster; it is not an exact coordinate for Penn School. Distances and boundary geometry are explicitly not to scale.

All 12 activities cite visible source summaries with resolved IDs. All targets have at least one detail. Timelines have distinct ascending years; placement is followed by a separate explanation. All evidence boards use at least two source IDs. The final E lessons compare perspectives and purposes rather than merely collecting unrelated facts.

All quiz answers are supported in their own linked teaching card's text, example, or tip. The shared authoring helper carries those notes into the persistent Quick Check reference and derives inline checks from the first question assigned to each card. Activity-only material is not required to answer a quiz question. Every question is explicitly authored; the helper only assembles IDs and presentation.
