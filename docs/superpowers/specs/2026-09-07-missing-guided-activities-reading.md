## R1 — Phrase Pathfinder

**Placement:** `reading-u01-l01-c2` in `reading-u01-l01`, *Read Accurately at a Good Pace*. Standard: `ELA.4.F.4.2`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Accurate words and meaningful phrase groups protect meaning; compare a practice copy with the original and mark useful reading pauses; see a corrected, annotated reading plan; explain what the words say and reflect on one's own practice.

**Complete source — The Winding Path:**

> Before the rain began, Isla carried the seedlings along the winding path. She stopped beside the wooden gate to check the labels. The tray marked “shade” belonged under the oak, but the tray marked “sun” belonged beside the fence. Isla reread both labels before putting the trays down.

**Loop:** The immutable original appears beside a clearly labeled “Practice copy with a changed word” using *windy path*. Select the mismatch and repair it from the original → add movable phrase boundaries after words, with preview highlighting one learner-chosen group at a time → read independently, silently or aloud → choose the detail explaining why Isla reread the labels → complete Accuracy / Pace / Meaning self-reflection. Optional standard TTS can read the original; phrase highlighting is a manual reading aid, not a measurement of speech.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “A changed word can change the picture. Let's make a reading plan that keeps the words and their meaning together.” Kid: “I’ll check the copy, mark useful word groups, and reread to explain what happened.” Start: **Plan my reading**.

**Coaching:** Wrong repair: “Compare every letter with the original sentence.” After phrase preview: “Try reading each group as one connected idea. Move a pause if it interrupts the meaning.” Unsupported meaning answer: “Look for the two labels and where each tray belongs.” Finish: “You checked the words and reflected on how your reading plan supported meaning.”

**Finish/key:** Repair *windy* to *winding*; connect shade/oak and sun/fence to label checking; complete the three reflection prompts. Phrase placement is exploratory: offer an optional sample after commitment, not one graded segmentation. Finish means practice/reflection completed, never “your fluency is accurate” or “your pace passed.”

**Build/verify:** New reading-rehearsal family. Preserve source text while annotating. Test arbitrary supported phrase plans, no microphone/timer/voice score, and that written-copy correction is not described as detecting the child's spoken error.

## R2 — Direct the Reading

**Placement:** `reading-u01-l02-c2` in `reading-u01-l02`, *Read with Expression and Intonation*. Standard: `ELA.4.F.4.2`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Expression follows textual clues; attach emphasis, pause, and voice-direction annotations; compare two possible annotated readings; explain how a clue supports a choice.

**Complete source — The Missing Kite:**

> “You found my kite?” Leo asked, staring at the muddy bundle. Ana lifted its bright tail. “I found it!” she said. Leo ran his fingers along the unbroken frame. “At last,” he whispered. “I thought the wind had carried it away.”

**Loop:** Select a line and highlight a clue → choose an emphasis word, a pause, and a tone label → preview the annotation visually → make a second reading choice for a different line → compare what the markings suggest about uncertainty, excitement, or relief → read independently and complete Text clue → Voice choice → Meaning connection. Keep the whole scene visible; a source quote remains beside each chosen annotation. Annotation preview does not promise that a device's TTS performs those prosodic choices.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “The words and punctuation give us clues about how these speakers feel.” Kid: “I’ll choose a clue, mark a possible voice choice, and explain the meaning it brings out.” Start: **Direct a reading**.

**Coaching:** Unsupported loudness claim: “Leo whispers. Which choice could show feeling without making his voice louder?” First annotated line: “Keep that choice. Compare it with a different line.” Reflection: “Explain what your selected words suggest, not just the name of a feeling.” Finish: “You connected two reading choices to clues in the scene.”

**Finish/key:** Two evidence-linked plans and a completed self-reflection. Accept question/uncertainty or surprise for “You found my kite?”, excitement for “I found it!”, and relief or gentle disbelief for “At last” with supporting context. No rigid question-always-rises rule. A child's performance is not checked or scored.

**Build/verify:** Second mode of R1's rehearsal family with emphasis/tone annotations. Test multiple authored valid interpretations, unchanged punctuation, optional audio unavailable, and honest completion copy.

## R3 — Word Desk

**Placement:** `reading-u02-l03-c3` in `reading-u02-l03`, *Use Print and Digital References Precisely*. Standard: `ELA.4.AOR.7.1`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Select the reference and meaning that fit a sentence; inspect entries and substitute a proposed meaning; compare the resulting sentence with its context; link entry evidence to context evidence.

**Complete source — Invented Cram All reference packet:**

> Field note: “We rested on the bank beside the creek. Near the coast, the creek water became brackish.”
>
> Dictionary: **bank** /bangk/, noun. 1. a business that keeps and lends money. 2. land along a river or stream.
>
> Topic glossary, Creek Habitats: **brackish**, adjective — slightly salty, as where fresh water and seawater mix.
>
> Reference information: Both entries were written for this Cram All practice packet. A dictionary can supply pronunciation, word class, and multiple meanings. A glossary explains words in a particular text or topic.

**Loop:** Choose dictionary or topic glossary for each task → select a relevant entry field → try a definition inside the field-note sentence → highlight context words supporting that choice → retain a two-row word notebook. The bank entry deliberately puts the unsuitable meaning first. A display switch offers “Page view” and “Search view” over exactly the same local packet; it performs no online search and makes no claim about a real publisher.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “The first meaning in an entry may not fit our sentence. Let's check the word desk.” Kid: “I’ll choose a reference, try a meaning in context, and show why it fits.” Start: **Open the word desk**.

**Coaching:** First-definition mistake: “Does a place that keeps money fit beside this creek?” Wrong reference field: “Which part of the entry explains meaning, rather than how to say the word?” First row complete: “Use the topic glossary for the next word's habitat meaning.” Finish: “You used both an entry and its sentence to choose precise meanings.”

**Finish/key:** bank meaning 2, supported by “beside the creek”; brackish means slightly salty, linked to coastal water. Learner also identifies bank as a noun and locates its pronunciation. Require both notebook rows; typing a meaning without source/context links is insufficient.

**Build/verify:** New reference-desk task sharing source selection patterns. Existing context-clue widget lacks entry fields/substitution. Test entry order, identical content in both views, and no external lookup dependency.

## R4 — Connect the Weather Report

**Placement:** `reading-u07-l02-c3` in `reading-u07-l02`, *Explain How Visuals and Multimedia Add Meaning*. Standard: `ELA.4.AOR.10.1`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Different media contribute different information; link a claim to a table cell, mapped location, and transcript phrase; see an annotated report; explain what each representation adds.

**Complete source — Invented practice media packet, Garden Rain Watch:**

> Report: Rain reached the west garden before the east garden on Monday morning.
>
> Rain collected, in inches: at 8 a.m., West 0.3 and East 0.0; at noon, West 0.9 and East 0.4.
>
> Map description: West Garden is on the left; East Garden is on the right. An arrow labeled “storm direction” points from West to East. At noon, West is labeled 0.9 inch and East 0.4 inch.
>
> Caption: A puddle surrounds the west garden's stepping-stone at noon.
>
> Supplied reporter transcript: “The west roof began pattering before breakfast. By noon, drops drummed loudly overhead. At the east garden, I heard softer tapping.”

**Loop:** Choose what to investigate—timing, amount, or described sound → inspect linked map/table/transcript panes → pin three source details onto a “Words say / Display adds / Together” board → build a combined explanation and identify what cannot be inferred. Render the actual simple map and table from the same data as their accessible descriptions. No pretend sound-playback button is supplied.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “The report, map, and transcript each tell us something different about the rain.” Kid: “I’ll pin exact details and explain what they add when I read them together.” Start: **Connect the report**.

**Coaching:** Vague evidence: “Choose a labeled value or exact phrase so a reader can check your idea.” Treating transcript as a measurement: “A sound description tells about the experience. Which display gives inches?” First links: “Keep both kinds of information in your explanation.” Finish: “You combined exact amounts, location, and described sound without mixing up what each source shows.”

**Finish/key:** Timing supported by 8 a.m. values; quantities by table; spatial relationship by map; experienced sound by transcript. Require three valid links covering quantity and at least one other contribution. Reject “louder means exactly twice as much rain” and claims of having heard supplied audio.

**Build/verify:** New media-evidence mode in a source-board family. Test table/map data parity, source availability, and faithful transcript-only labels.

## R5 — Author's Lens

**Placement:** `reading-u08-l01-c3` in `reading-u08-l01`, *Connect Author's Purpose and Perspective*. Standard: `ELA.4.AOR.4.1`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Wording reveals a viewpoint and helps achieve an author's purpose; highlight language in paired texts and connect evidence labels; observe two completed language→perspective→purpose chains; explain their difference.

**Complete sources — Two texts about a book bench:**

> **A: A Place to Read.** I believe our school should put a reading bench beside the library. Right now, readers sit on the hard steps while waiting for the doors to open. A bench would give them a comfortable place to share books. It would need space and regular care, but I think those jobs are worth doing. Please ask the school council to consider a bench.
>
> **B: Planning a Reading Bench.** A reading bench provides seating near a place where books are used. Planning begins with the space available and the number of seats needed. Paths must stay clear, and the bench needs regular checks. Different sites have different needs. These points describe what a school would consider before placing a bench.

**Loop:** Mark two phrases in each complete text → attach possible perspective and purpose labels → commit and compare the chains → explain how a supportive request differs from a neutral explanation. The evidence board keeps selected wording verbatim. The learner does not rewrite source text or classify the author by first/third-person pronouns alone.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “Both authors discuss a bench, but they want different things from their readers.” Kid: “I’ll mark their language and connect it to a viewpoint and a purpose.” Start: **Compare the authors**.

**Coaching:** Topic-only response: “Both texts mention a bench. What does this author want the reader to do or understand?” Pronoun-only reasoning: “Look for judgments, requests, or explanations beyond a pronoun.” Valid chain: “Keep the quoted words connected to your interpretation.” Finish: “You explained how each author's language helps carry out a purpose.”

**Finish/key:** A supports installing a bench and advocates consideration, with belief/request evidence; B uses neutral planning detail to explain considerations. Require two complete chains and one comparison. Accept equivalent phrasing from bounded evidence-supported options, not a guessed feeling about the author.

**Build/verify:** New purpose/perspective mode in the source-board family. The existing POV pronoun converter is not suitable. Test evidence attribution to the correct text and rejection of topic-as-purpose.

## R6 — Build the Support Chain

**Placement:** `reading-u08-l02-c3` in `reading-u08-l02`, *Explain Claims, Reasons, and Evidence*. Standard: `ELA.4.AOR.5.3`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Evidence supports a reason that supports a claim; connect source strips into chains; see which statements provide support and which are irrelevant or limited; explain without overstating the source.

**Complete source — Keep the Book-Return Tray, invented practice argument:**

> Our classroom should keep the book-return tray beside the door. It gives readers one clear place to leave finished books. A classroom log recorded 84 returns in the tray during six weeks.
>
> The tray may also help the class find returned books. During the week before it was added, six returned books were found on other shelves. In the first week with the tray, two were found elsewhere. The weeks differed, so the counts alone do not prove the tray caused the change.
>
> The tray has a yellow star painted on it. It must be emptied regularly so it does not overflow. With that care, it is worth keeping.

**Loop:** Pull the claim strip into the center → link two reason strips to it → attach the relevant log/count strips → park unrelated decoration separately → add a “Limit” tag to the before/after comparison. Links are semantic relationships, not an arbitrary sentence order. All source paragraphs remain visible.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “A useful detail needs a connection to the author's claim. Let's build those connections.” Kid: “I’ll link evidence to reasons, keep unrelated details aside, and include the source's limit.” Start: **Build the support chain**.

**Coaching:** Claim repeated as reason: “That repeats what the author wants. Find a statement explaining why.” Irrelevant detail: “Does the star tell us whether the tray helps readers return books?” Overclaim: “The source says the weeks differed. Keep that limit with your comparison.” Completion: “Your chains show how the evidence supports the claim and what it cannot prove.”

**Finish/key:** 84 returns → clear shared return place → keep tray; six versus two elsewhere → possible organization benefit → keep tray, with causal limit. Yellow star is irrelevant; regular emptying is a maintenance condition, not irrelevant decoration. Require both chains and correct treatment of limit/condition.

**Build/verify:** New argument mode in the source-board family; do not flatten it into main-idea matching. Test reason/evidence distinction, maintenance condition, and retaining the qualification.

## R7 — Two Views, One Event

**Placement:** `reading-u09-l02-c3` in `reading-u09-l02`, *Explain How Character Perspectives Shape a Story*. Standard: `ELA.4.AOR.3.1`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Different interpretations of the same event shape reactions and later events; construct parallel viewpoint chains from source strips; observe where misunderstanding begins and changes; explain the effect on the story.

**Complete source — The Moved Rehearsal:**

> Rain moved the music rehearsal from the courtyard into a small room. Ari frowned. He had practiced a drum part that sounded best in the open space. “Now no one will hear it properly,” he said.
>
> Bea smiled and carried her violin inside. “At least the instruments will stay dry.” Ari thought her smile meant she did not care about his drum part, so he stopped helping arrange the chairs.
>
> Bea noticed. “I know you wanted the courtyard,” she told him. “I'm relieved about the rain, not happy that you're disappointed.” Ari nodded. He suggested moving the chairs farther from the drum. Bea helped, and they tried a quieter version of the part together.

**Loop:** Pin the shared event → select separate Ari and Bea evidence strips → connect viewpoint to reaction and then story consequence → mark the line that changes Ari's interpretation → compare the beginning and ending chains. No pretend chat with real people, invented additional ending, or single “correct personality” label.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “The rehearsal changed for everyone, but Ari and Bea see that change differently.” Kid: “I’ll trace each view to a reaction and show how the misunderstanding changes.” Start: **Trace the two views**.

**Coaching:** Mixed viewpoints: “Whose words or action does that strip describe?” Unsupported motive: “Use what the story says instead of adding a new reason.” Turning point found: “Compare what Ari thought before Bea spoke with what he does afterward.” Completion: “You connected different perspectives to the conflict and the later cooperation.”

**Finish/key:** Ari's disappointment → assumes Bea does not care → stops helping; Bea's relief about keeping instruments dry → clarification → shared adjustment/cooperation. Require both evidence-linked chains and the turning-point link. Reject interpretation of Bea's smile as proof she dislikes Ari.

**Build/verify:** New character-perspective mode of the source-board family. Reuse story-map interaction patterns; do not reuse pronoun conversion as a perspective lesson. Test order-independent valid evidence and no editorial answer paragraph in the source.

## R8 — One Moment, Three Forms

**Placement:** `reading-u10-l01-c3` in `reading-u10-l01`, *Compare Narratives, Dramas, and Poems*. Standard: `ELA.4.AOR.5.1`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Literary forms present a shared event through different structures; align matching moments and mark form-specific features; compare how those features carry action or emphasis; explain the effect of one structural choice.

**Complete sources — The Red Mitten:**

> **Narrative**
>
> June found a red mitten under the library bench. She held it up as a boy hurried back through the door. “That's mine,” he said. June passed it to him, and his worried face relaxed.

> **One-scene drama**
>
> [A library doorway. June lifts a red mitten from beneath a bench.]
>
> BOY: That's mine!
>
> [June hands him the mitten. He lets out a long breath.]
>
> JUNE: Just in time for the cold walk home.

> **Free-verse poem**
>
> Under the bench,\
> one red mitten waits—\
> a small warm promise.
>
> A searching boy,\
> an open hand,\
> a worry set down.

**Loop:** Align the discovery/return/relief moments across the three sources → pin paragraph, speaker-label, stage-direction, line, and stanza markers to actual examples → choose two forms to keep side by side → explain how a narrator tells relief, a stage direction makes it performable, or a poem compresses it into “a worry set down.” Preserve poem line breaks and stanza gaps; do not turn it into prose in the source pane.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “The same mitten changes hands in three forms. Each form helps us notice something.” Kid: “I’ll match the shared moment, mark the structures, and compare how they tell it.” Start: **Compare the three forms**.

**Coaching:** Genre from topic: “The mitten appears in all three. Look at how speech and action are arranged.” Misplaced feature: “Speaker labels and bracketed actions belong to the performance text.” First alignment: “Now explain what one form's structure lets the reader notice.” Completion: “You connected structure to how each version presents the same moment.”

**Finish/key:** Require aligned return moment, at least one correct structural example per form, and one supported contrast. Do not demand identical details across versions; the drama adds a spoken line and the poem compresses events. Accept narrator/action-direction and paragraph/line-stanza comparisons.

**Build/verify:** New literary-form comparison mode using shared source panels. Test preserved whitespace, source attribution, and simultaneous availability of the two forms being compared.

## R9 — Literal and Vivid

**Placement:** `reading-u10-l03-c3` in `reading-u10-l03`, *Explain Figurative Language's Effect*. Standard: `ELA.4.AOR.1.2`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Figurative language changes imagery and feeling beyond literal facts; replace an authored phrase in a separate preview; compare original and literal versions; connect a reader effect to source context.

**Complete source — Before the Race:**

> At the starting line, a swarm of butterflies filled Noah's stomach. He pressed his shoes against the track and shook out his hands. Beside him, runners waited without speaking. Then the whistle sliced through the silence. Noah lifted his head and took his first quick step.

**Loop:** Select one of the two figurative spans → choose a faithful literal substitute → commit to see it in a labeled comparison copy → pin a context clue and an effect phrase → repeat with the second span → compare what the original adds. The original never changes. Each substitution is authored; no unconstrained generation or claim of automated literary interpretation.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “We could tell these events plainly, but the author chose vivid phrases. Let's see what those choices add.” Kid: “I’ll try a literal version, compare it with the original, and use the scene to explain the effect.” Start: **Compare the wording**.

**Coaching:** Literal misunderstanding: “Check the race setting. Are there actual insects in Noah's stomach?” Unfaithful substitute: “Keep the event the same while changing the wording.” Naming only the figure: “Describe the picture or feeling it creates for a reader.” Completion: “You explained what each phrase adds beyond the literal event.”

**Finish/key:** butterflies → nervous feeling, supported by waiting/shaking hands; whistle sliced silence → a sharp sound suddenly interrupts quiet. Accept anticipation/anxiety with supporting details; reject physical insects or an actual cut. Require literal meaning plus evidence-linked effect for both, not merely naming idiom/metaphor.

**Build/verify:** Extend figurative-language work with complete context, explicit literal comparison, and effect links; current phrase/kind/meaning matching alone is insufficient. Test source immutability and multiple supported effects.

## R10 — Question Compass

**Placement:** `reading-u11-l01-c3` in `reading-u11-l01`, *Ask an Inquiry Question and Examine a Source*. Standard: `ELA.4.R.1.1`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** A useful inquiry names what information is needed and respects a source's scope; build a bounded question, inspect headings, and collect linked findings; see supported findings alongside remaining questions; explain the source's limit.

**Complete source — A Classroom Reading Corner, invented planning notes:**

> **Location:** Place seating where readers have enough light and where bags do not block the walking path.
>
> **Books:** Offer a small mix of stories, poems, and information books. Display the covers so readers can see the choices.
>
> **Care:** Assign a regular time to straighten the books and check that seats and paths remain clear.
>
> **Limits:** These notes describe design choices. They contain no prices, no survey of favorite books, and no measurements of how much students will read.

**Loop:** Assemble a question from topic + information-needed tiles → scan the complete source and bookmark headings → pull two or three relevant source sentences into a notebook → match each to an accurate own-words finding → move an unanswerable question to “Need another source.” The learner may revise the inquiry; revision triggers a new relevance check instead of silently keeping stale answers.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “A focused question helps us notice useful information. One source may not answer everything.” Kid: “I’ll build a question, collect findings that answer it, and mark what I still need to learn.” Start: **Set my inquiry**.

**Coaching:** Overbroad question: “Which part of a reading corner do you want to understand?” Unsupported finding: “Find the sentence that supports this note.” Source limit: “Keep that question for another source; this packet does not provide its answer.” Completion: “You gathered source-linked findings for a focused question and named a limit.”

**Finish/key:** Accept “What choices could help us set up a classroom reading corner?” with findings from location/books/care, or “How could we keep the corner ready for readers?” with paths/book-care findings. Require at least two relevant linked findings and a genuine missing-information question such as cost or favorite titles. Never infer popularity or a reading increase.

**Build/verify:** New inquiry mode in the source-board family. Candidate question variants have explicit allowed evidence sets. Test source changes after question revision and the separation of a supported finding from an unanswered question.

## R11 — Pack the Research Folder

**Placement:** `reading-u11-l03-c3` in `reading-u11-l03`, *Select Information Relevant to a Topic*. Standard: `ELA.4.R.1.3`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Relevance depends on the question, not truth or preference; collect and reject note cards with source links; inspect a folder containing only useful findings; explain one inclusion and one exclusion.

**Complete source — Invented classroom research notes:**

> Question: Which classroom actions could reduce paper being thrown away after only one side is used?
>
> Note A: Keep a tray of clean one-sided sheets for rough sketches and practice work.
>
> Note B: The classroom door is painted green.
>
> Note C: Check whether both sides of a practice sheet can be used before taking a new one.
>
> Note D: Count one-sided sheets in the paper bin for a week to see how often this happens.
>
> Note E: The class mascot is an otter.
>
> Note F: Add a “Clean reverse sides for practice” label to the reuse tray.

**Loop:** Underline the exact information needed → move notes into “Helps answer” or “Doesn't answer this question” → tag useful notes as action or investigation → pin a reason to one accepted and one rejected note → change to the supplied second question “What does our classroom look like?” and reconsider B. Do not require all notes to be useful under some question; the transfer specifically shows why B's relevance changes.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “A true detail can still miss our question. Our research folder needs useful connections.” Kid: “I’ll sort the notes by the question, explain my choices, and check what changes with a new question.” Start: **Pack the research folder**.

**Coaching:** Preference-based inclusion: “Does liking that detail help answer the paper-use question?” Audit confusion: “A count can help investigate a problem even though counting alone does not fix it.” Valid first folder: “Now watch how a new question changes one note's usefulness.” Completion: “You judged relevance by the question and explained why a detail could change groups.”

**Finish/key:** A/C/F are actions; D is useful investigation, not proof of reduced waste; B/E are irrelevant to the original question. B becomes relevant to appearance. Require the whole first sort, two links, and the second-question revision.

**Build/verify:** Research-folder mode, sharing card controls with R12. Test neutral note IDs/order and that an investigation is not mislabeled as a demonstrated result.

## R12 — Research Clusters

**Placement:** `reading-u11-l04-c3` in `reading-u11-l04`, *Group Related Research Findings*. Standard: `ELA.4.R.1.4`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Group findings by a shared idea and describe the pattern; arrange notes in clusters, label them, and build a group statement; see how several findings support a broader idea; explain grouping rather than inventing a sequence.

**Complete source — Invented findings, A Useful Classroom Library:**

> Note A: Story, poetry, and information labels help readers locate a kind of book.
>
> Note B: Smooth page turns can help keep pages from tearing.
>
> Note C: Each reading circle member gets a turn to describe a favorite part.
>
> Note D: A title list helps readers check which shelf holds a book.
>
> Note E: A dry place for returned books helps protect the pages.
>
> Note F: Readers can leave a short recommendation for someone choosing a book.

**Loop:** Inspect all six notes → create three clusters using selectable folder outlines → name the shared idea from “Finding books / Caring for books / Sharing reading” labels → move notes and commit → connect each group to a proportional synthesis sentence. Folder position and note order are free; an optional own-word category label is reflective and not automatically graded. Keep full note text readable after placement.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “Several findings can point to one bigger idea. Let's arrange them so another reader can see the connections.” Kid: “I’ll group related notes, name each shared idea, and explain what the group shows.” Start: **Build research clusters**.

**Coaching:** One group per note: “Look for a second note that shares the same main idea.” Chronological explanation: “These notes describe related ideas. They do not tell us what happened first.” Completed placement: “Name what both notes in each group suggest together.” Completion: “Your groups turn separate findings into three clear shared ideas.”

**Finish/key:** A/D finding books; B/E caring for books; C/F sharing reading. Require all six notes placed once and three faithful group statements. Accept any spatial/order arrangement. If learners add a cross-link, retain it as a secondary connection without falsely declaring the primary relationship impossible.

**Build/verify:** Grouping mode shares folder controls with R11, but checks group synthesis rather than relevance. Source IDs carry no category prefix. Test arbitrary order, duplicate prevention, full source labels, and non-scoring of free writing.

## R13 — Give the Source Credit

**Placement:** `reading-u11-l05-c3` in `reading-u11-l05`, *Cite Sources and Avoid Plagiarism*. Standard: `ELA.4.R.1.5`.

**Concept → action → result → reasoning:** Borrowed wording and ideas need faithful handling and credit; repair a quotation and construct a citation from provided fields; compare quotation, paraphrase, and patchwriting; explain why both quotation and paraphrase still name a source.

**Complete source — Invented practice publication, not a real publication:**

> Author: Lena Moss\
> Title: A Shelf for Every Reader\
> Publisher: Cram All Practice Library\
> Year: 2026
>
> Source text: “Clear shelf labels help readers find books without searching every row. A short title list can also guide a reader to the correct shelf. Labels need checking when books move.”
>
> For this activity, use: Author — Title — Publisher — Year.

**Loop:** In a separate draft, inspect the exact copied sentence “Labels need checking when books move.” shown without quote marks or credit → add quotation marks and an attribution → assemble four citation fields from source metadata → compare two paraphrase drafts → attach the same source credit to the faithful draft. Draft A: “Moss explains that labels and a title list can guide readers to books, and labels may need updating after books are moved.” Draft B: “Clear shelf tags help readers find books without looking through every row.” The original and metadata stay visible throughout.

**Dialogue:** Winnie: “We can use a source's words or explain its idea in our own wording. Both need credit.” Kid: “I’ll repair a quotation, build the citation from the source, and check a paraphrase.” Start: **Repair the source credit**.

**Coaching:** Missing marks: “Those are the source's exact words. Show where the quotation begins and ends.” Invented metadata: “Use the four details supplied here; do not fill gaps by guessing.” Patchwriting: “Changing a few words keeps the source's sentence shape. Compare the draft that explains the idea in a new structure.” Completion: “You marked borrowed wording and gave credit for both the quotation and the paraphrase.”
